# Model the Situation

Construct a fit-for-purpose map of reality that makes the user's choice or situation easier to understand. Keep facts, interpretations, assumptions, forecasts, values, and authority visibly distinct. Treat the model as revisable, not as reality itself.

## Calibrate the Work

Match depth to stakes and ambiguity:

- Use a compact orientation for simple, low-stakes questions.
- Use the standard model for consequential or multi-party situations.
- Use a deep model with research, competing frames, causal structure, and scenarios when stakes, uncertainty, novelty, or irreversibility are high.

Do not manufacture a decision when the user needs orientation, diagnosis, monitoring, or explanation. Do not delay useful modeling until every detail is known.

## Build the Working Model

### 1. Establish the model's job

Determine what the model must help the user do: understand, decide, diagnose, anticipate, explain, coordinate, negotiate, or act. Identify the focal question, decision owner, affected parties, time horizon, deadline or trigger, and relevant authority. Infer missing details when safe and label material assumptions; ask only for information whose absence would substantially change the model.

State the boundary: what is inside the model, what is adjacent, and what is deliberately excluded. Treat user examples as probes rather than a complete inventory.

### 2. Reconstruct the situation through time

Organize available information into:

- prior conditions and important transitions;
- current state and active forces;
- direction of travel, horizon events, and plausible discontinuities;
- direct observations or established facts;
- reported claims and their sources;
- inferences and assumptions;
- important unknowns.

For changing facts, niche domains, high-stakes matters, source-specific requests, or material gaps, research before concluding. Search to answer explicit model gaps. Prefer primary or official sources, then high-quality research or domain reporting. Compare sources when interests or interpretations differ, timestamp volatile claims, and cite claims near the text they support. Never use research as decoration or imply that evidence settles a value judgment.

### 3. Map the structure

Select only dimensions that change understanding:

- entities, actors, roles, and affected groups;
- goals, incentives, fears, commitments, and conflicts;
- resources, capabilities, dependencies, and bottlenecks;
- rules, constraints, permissions, obligations, and vetoes;
- causal mechanisms, feedback loops, delays, and thresholds;
- information flows and asymmetries;
- state, trajectory, decision points, and path dependence;
- uncertainties, hazards, recoverability, and observables.

Classify the context only when it affects the method: ordered, expert-analyzable, complex/adaptive, chaotic, or genuinely unclear. Avoid forcing a named framework onto the situation. Read [references/frameworks.md](references/frameworks.md) when the situation is complex, a framework choice matters, or a deeper challenge pass is needed.

### 4. Test and reframe

Challenge the first coherent story. Look for anomalies, missing actors, hidden authority, competing incentives, base rates, delayed effects, feedback, second-order consequences, and evidence that would falsify the current frame. Construct at least one materially different frame when interpretation is contested or confidence is low.

State:

- what the current frame explains well;
- what it fails to explain;
- the strongest competing explanation;
- what observation would distinguish them;
- what would change the recommendation or model.

Do not substitute a generic bias checklist for a concrete challenge.

### 5. Add a decision layer when a real choice exists

Separate the situation model from the decision model. Make explicit:

- the decision actually being made, by whom, and by when;
- what is fixed versus controllable;
- objectives, values, tradeoffs, and unacceptable outcomes;
- viable alternatives, including delay, no action, delegation, and information-gathering when genuinely available;
- expected consequences, uncertainties, reversibility, option value, and downstream commitments;
- the smallest safe experiment or information gain that could change the choice;
- recommendation, confidence, assumptions, and review triggers when advice is requested.

Distinguish decision quality from outcome quality: uncertainty permits a sound decision to have a bad outcome. Do not claim authority the user or another decision owner retains.

## Present the Model

Lead with a plain-language orientation: **what this is and why it matters now**. Then use the smallest representation that exposes the important relationships. Prefer a short timeline for sequence, a table for exact comparisons, a causal map for feedback, a tree for contingent choices, or prose for a simple model.

Adapt this default structure rather than filling it mechanically:

1. **Orientation** — the situation in one to three sentences.
2. **Model boundary and purpose** — what this model covers and enables.
3. **How it works** — actors, structure, causal forces, and timeline.
4. **Known / inferred / unknown** — provenance and confidence.
5. **Tensions and competing frames** — what makes the situation difficult.
6. **Decision layer** — only if a decision exists.
7. **Plausible futures and signposts** — what may happen and what to watch.
8. **Next move** — action, research, experiment, or question with highest value.

For a compact answer, collapse this to: **what it is; what drives it; what is uncertain; what the user is deciding; what matters next**.

## Quality Gate

Before delivering, verify that:

- the output makes the scenario more legible rather than merely longer;
- facts, interpretations, forecasts, and preferences are distinguishable;
- unknown names, jurisdictions, dates, and other specifics remain unknown rather than being filled from unrelated ambient context;
- the model includes time, agency, authority, uncertainty, and consequences when relevant;
- sparse examples did not silently define the whole domain;
- a preferred option did not distort the frame or eliminate alternatives;
- research addressed decision-relevant uncertainty and has usable citations;
- confidence and model-breaking evidence are explicit;
- the answer stays proportional and does not bury the user in framework terminology.
